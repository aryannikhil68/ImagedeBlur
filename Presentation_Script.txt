SLIDE 2: INTRODUCTION TO IMAGE DEBLURRING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[SLIDE CONTENT]:
- The Real-World Problem: Shaky hands, fast-moving objects, optical defocus.
- What Happens at the Pixel Level: Light smears across adjacent sensor pixels.
- The Mathematical Blur Model: B = (I * K) + N
  * B: Observed blurry image
  * I: Hidden true sharp image
  * K: Blur Kernel (Point Spread Function - PSF)
  * *: 2D Convolution operation
  * N: Random camera sensor noise
- The Core Challenge: Deblurring is an 'ill-posed inverse problem.'

[WHAT SPEAKER 1 SHOULD SAY]:
"To understand deblurring, let us look at what happens inside a digital camera. When you press the shutter button, the sensor collects light for a fraction of a second. If the camera shakes or the subject moves during that tiny window, the light rays from a single point do not land on a single pixel—they smear across dozens of neighboring pixels.

In digital image processing, we write this down with a simple mathematical equation:
B equals I convolved with K, plus N.
Here, 'B' is the blurry photo we can see. 'I' is the true, clean image that we wish we had captured. 'K' is the blur kernel, technically known as the Point Spread Function, which represents the exact trajectory of the camera shake. And 'N' is random sensor noise.

Now, why is deblurring so difficult? Mathematicians classify deblurring as an 'ill-posed inverse problem.' It is ill-posed because once high-frequency details like sharp edges, fine textures, or hair strands are blurred together, they are lost. Mathematically, there are infinitely many possible sharp scenes that could have produced that single blurry photograph. Naively trying to reverse the equation amplifies the sensor noise and produces meaningless static."

Our pipeline operates in five clear stages:
Stage 1: High-contrast, structured scenes with rich geometric edges are generated.
Stage 2: Authentic 2D Point Spread Function motion blur kernels, ranging from 7 to 13 pixels across random angles, convolve the scenes to generate paired blurry counterparts.
Stage 3: The pairs are fed into our custom PyTorch DeblurNet-Lite model in small batches of 4.
Stage 4: Backpropagation computes the L1 restoration loss between the network's prediction and the true sharp target, adjusting the synaptic weights.
Stage 5: The final model is saved as a checkpoint and evaluated on unseen test data.

By constraining our training patches to 128x128 and our batch size to 4, our entire training pipeline operates inside just 380 megabytes of RAM—completely preventing any out-of-memory errors on our 4 GB laptop."

[WHAT SPEAKER 2 SHOULD SAY]:
"A common mistake in machine learning projects is data leakage, where parts of the test data accidentally influence training. We ensured zero data leakage: our 300 scenes were strictly partitioned into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing based on unique scene IDs before any blur was applied.

Our preprocessing pipeline consists of three vital steps:
First, Bicubic Resizing: All images are resized to 128x128 pixels using bicubic interpolation, which prevents jagged pixel edges.
Second, Normalization: Standard image files store pixels as 8-bit integers from 0 to 255. We divide all pixel values by 255.0 to map them into floating-point numbers between 0.0 and 1.0. This prevents exploding gradients during backpropagation.
Third, Synchronized Data Augmentation: To prevent our model from simply memorizing training images, we apply random horizontal and vertical flips. Crucially, the flip must be applied identically to both the blurry image and the sharp image at the exact same moment. If you flip one and not the other, the pixel alignment is broken and the model fails to learn."

SLIDE 7: OUR CUSTOM CNN ARCHITECTURE (DeblurNet-Lite)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[SLIDE CONTENT]:
- Architectural Blueprint: Compact Residual Convolutional Autoencoder.
- Total Trainable Parameters: Exactly 293,642 (under 300K).
- Three Core Components:
  1. Encoder: 3x3 Conv layers (3 -> 32 -> 64 channels) with stride-2 downsampling.
  2. Bottleneck: 3 consecutive Residual Blocks with identity shortcuts (x + F(x)).
  3. Decoder: Transposed Convolution (stride 2) + Symmetric Skip Connection + Conv 3x3.
  4. Output Activation: Sigmoid layer ensuring valid [0.0, 1.0] pixel values.
- Weight Initialization: Random He / Kaiming Normal initialization (Strictly no pretrained weights).

[WHAT SPEAKER 2 SHOULD SAY]:
"On this slide, you see the architectural blueprint of our custom model, which we named DeblurNet-Lite. It contains exactly 293,642 trainable parameters. That is less than 1% of the size of commercial models, yet it retains all essential restoration mechanics.

DeblurNet-Lite consists of three key sections:
First, the Encoder: It takes the 3-channel RGB blurry image and passes it through 3x3 convolutions, expanding it to 32 and then 64 feature channels while downsampling spatial dimensions. This allows the network to capture large-scale blur patterns.

Second, the Bottleneck: It contains three stacked Residual Blocks. Each residual block contains two convolution layers with Batch Normalization and PReLU activation, plus an identity skip shortcut: Output equals Input plus F(Input). Residual connections allow gradients to flow backwards through deep layers without vanishing, allowing the network to learn subtle edge corrections.

Third, the Decoder: A transposed convolution upsamples the feature maps back to full 128x128 resolution. Here, we introduce our Symmetric Skip Connection: we concatenate the high-resolution features from the very first layer directly with the decoded features. This is critical because downsampling loses fine edges; the skip connection feeds those spatial edges directly back into the final reconstruction layer.

Finally, a 3x3 convolution and Sigmoid activation constrain the output strictly between 0.0 and 1.0. All weights were initialized randomly using He Normal initialization."

SLIDE 8: LOSS FUNCTION AND OPTIMIZATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[SLIDE CONTENT]:
- Loss Function: L1 Mean Absolute Error (MAE)
  Formula: Loss = (1 / N) * sum(|Predicted - GroundTruth|)
- Why L1 Loss instead of L2 (MSE)?
  * L2 squares large errors, causing the model to average out edge discontinuities, producing blurry, smooth results.
  * L1 penalizes errors linearly, preserving sharp boundaries and fine lines.
- Optimizer: Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation)
  * Learning Rate: 0.001
  * Weight Decay: 1e-5 (L2 regularization to prevent overfitting)
  * Learning Rate Scheduler: ReduceLROnPlateau (halves LR when validation loss plateaus)

[WHAT SPEAKER 2 SHOULD SAY]:
"In college vivas, professors frequently ask: 'Why did you choose L1 loss instead of standard Mean Squared Error (L2)?'

Here is the exact scientific reason: Mean Squared Error squares the difference between pixels. When an edge is slightly misplaced, MSE produces a massive penalty. To minimize this penalty, the network takes the mathematical average of all possible edge locations, which creates an over-smoothed, blurry image! 

L1 loss, on the other hand, measures the absolute error linearly. It does not over-penalize sharp transitions. Therefore, L1 loss encourages the network to produce crisp, sharp edges.

To optimize the weights, we used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and weight decay for regularization. We also integrated a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler that automatically reduces the learning rate by half if the validation loss stops improving.
